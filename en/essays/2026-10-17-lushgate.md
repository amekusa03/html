# Automatic Field Sprinkler System: Off to the Field—And the "Siphon" Trap That Stands in the Way

2026-10-17 / ESP32 / IoT / LushGate

Our automatic sprinkler system, “LushGate,” has completed bench testing after we thoroughly optimized the circuit for power efficiency, switched to dry-cell batteries as the power source, and migrated communication to Web Bluetooth.

I had been stuck at the stage where all that was left was to take it to the site, but the garden hose—whose delivery had been delayed—finally arrived.
I couldn’t wait any longer, so I immediately took it to the garden to set it up and test the water flow.

## The Long-Awaited On-Site Deployment

I placed the LushGate unit, which I’d put in a Tupperware container, next to the rainwater tank in the garden and submerged the pump at the bottom of the tank. I ran the sprinkler hose I’d just received along the garden beds, and that completed the setup.

Connect to LushGate via Web Bluetooth using your smartphone's browser, then tap the "Manual Watering Test" button on the screen.

"Click"

Accompanied by the soothing sound of the relay clicking, the kerosene pump began to hum smoothly.
A few seconds later, water began to spray from each nozzle on the hose, evenly moistening the dry soil. I could check the watering status on my smartphone’s UI—everything was working exactly as designed.

"Perfect. Now the fields in the mountains are fully automated, too."

But my relief was short-lived, and my sense of satisfaction didn't last long either.

## The pump stopped, but the water won't stop flowing.

The set watering time had elapsed, the relay had turned off, and the sound of the pump had stopped completely.
...Or so it should have, but for some reason, water kept trickling out of the end of the watering hose.

"Huh? Did the relay weld shut and get stuck in the 'ON' position?"

I hurriedly opened the Tupperware lid to check the indicator, but the ESP32 had successfully transitioned to the Light Sleep standby state, and the relay was definitely OFF. Naturally, power to the pump had been completely cut off.

And yet, the water gushing from the hose shows no sign of stopping. If anything, it even seems to be gaining momentum as it falls under the force of gravity.

If we leave things as they are, it will be a disaster: the precious water stored in the rainwater tank will keep flowing out until it’s completely empty after just one watering.

## The cause is the "siphon effect."

For a split second, I wondered, “Is it a problem with the valve inside the pump? Or is it negative pressure caused by Pascal’s principle?” But the moment I noticed the difference in height between the tank and the hose, a phenomenon familiar from science experiments flashed through my mind.

**The principle of the siphon (siphon phenomenon)**.

The outlet of the sprinkler hose, which runs along the ground in the field, is much lower than the water level in the rainwater tank.
Once the pump fills the hose with water at the start of watering, it doesn’t matter if you stop the pump or turn off the power partway through. Due to the head difference (height difference) and the negative pressure created by the continuous column of water inside the hose, the water in the tank continues to be drawn out on its own by gravity.

I was so preoccupied with the microcontroller's power consumption and the BLE communication stack that I completely overlooked fluid dynamics—a classic and fundamental law of physics.

## The Most Primitive Yet Most Powerful Solution: The Siphon Breaker

As long as you understand the principle, you can take countermeasures. All you have to do is draw air into the hose to interrupt the water flow and break the chain of negative pressure. This is what’s known as a **siphon breaker**.

It would be overkill to go to the trouble of procuring and installing a check valve or a vacuum breaker. So, I decided to take the most down-to-earth—and most reliable—approach using the tools available on-site.

Above the water level in the tank—at the base of the sprinkler hose (right after the pump)—I poked a small hole to let air in.

While the pump is forcefully pressurizing the water, a small amount of water does spurt out of this tiny hole, but since it’s above the water level in the tank, it simply flows back in, causing no real harm.
Then, the moment the watering is finished and the pump stops—air is sucked in with a “whoosh” through that tiny hole, breaking the water column inside the hose and causing the siphon to collapse instantly.

The stream of water from the garden hose stopped exactly as intended.

## Let's start with a trial run.

No matter how cleverly we integrated high-tech BLE communication and microcontroller-based control, what ultimately proved most reliable in the field was a tiny hole poked in a hose with a needle. This hands-on experience might just be the true thrill of field testing—stepping beyond the confines of the lab.
