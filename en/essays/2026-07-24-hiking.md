# Who owns the hiking trail?

2026.07.24

Hiking trails (climbing trails and walking trails) are owned by a variety of people, including the national government, local governments, companies, and individuals. Some places have a long history and it is not clear whose land belongs to them. Many trails are supported by long history of use, goodwill by owners, and local management.

We have summarized the rules for passing on private land and how to identify the trails you will encounter while hiking.

## 1. Can I use private land?

In principle, you can pass with the owner's permission or good faith (acquiescence).

* Existence of right of way: It has a long history of being used as a community road and mountain trail.
* Good intentions of the owners: In most cases, they allow access out of kindness, saying, ``It's for the sake of hikers.''
* Route maintenance: Mountain huts and local mountaineering groups maintain the trails in consultation with owners.

### Cases that should not be entered

* No Trespassing Signs: If the owner specifically refuses to do so, you may be trespassing.
* Interfering with private life: It is prohibited to enter the immediate vicinity of private houses or agricultural land (fields, orchards) without permission.

## 2. Types of roads and how to distinguish them

This is the standard for determining whether the road in front of you is a place you can enter or private land that should be avoided.

### Fields/orchards (no entry allowed)

* How to tell: The soil is tilled, has ridges, fruit trees are in regular rows, and there are bird nets or fences.
* Features: 100% farmland (private land). To prevent crop diseases and theft, never enter the surrounding grounds, even if they look like roads.

### Mountain forests/climbing trails (generally allowed, exceptions may apply)

* How to tell: Trees are naturally growing. There are signposts (signboards) and pink tape (markers).
* Characteristics: Even if the trail is on private land, in most cases, if there is a clear mountain trail, it is ``tacit permission''. However, it is strictly prohibited to enter areas with "Mt. Matsutake" and "No Trespassing" signs.

### General road (anyone can pass)

* How to tell: Well-paved with asphalt. There are white lines (center lines and roadside strips) and road signs.
* Characteristics: It is a "public road" managed by the national or local government. Everyone can walk freely.

### Farm roads (in principle passable, priority given to work)

* How to tell: It runs weaving between fields. Simple concrete pavement or unpaved (gravel/soil).
* Characteristics: Most of the land is privately owned, but basically anyone can walk through it. Agricultural vehicles such as light trucks and tractors have the highest priority, so give way so as not to interfere with their work.

### Forest road (in principle passable, but many vehicles are prohibited)

* How to tell: A road that runs through the mountains. There is a signboard that says ``Forest Road 〇〇 Line.'' There is a gate and chain at the entrance.
* Features: A path for forest management. Pedestrians are often allowed to pass, but care must be taken against falling rocks and work vehicles. If the gate is closed, it usually means "vehicles are prohibited," but be sure to check the signboards to see if pedestrians are also prohibited.

## 3. Manners when walking on the road

These are basic rules to prevent mountain route closures and ensure everyone can walk comfortably.

* Stay on course: Only walk on designated paths to avoid trampling plants.
* Take your trash home: Littering on private property is the number one cause of route closures.
* Do not collect plants or animals: If you collect wild vegetables or mushrooms without permission, you may be charged with theft.
