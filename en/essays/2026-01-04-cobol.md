# COBOLer forever

2026-01-04

The story is long.

I have only learned COBOL.
To be exact, COBOL was the only course I took in class when I was a student.
But I started programming when I was a child.

## childhood

At that time, the Famicom was booming, and I wanted one too.
I played with it at a friend's house and wanted it so badly that I counted my pocket money.
From what I remember, the main unit costs about 15,000 yen and the cassette costs about 5,000 yen.
Maybe if you buy one cassette, you can buy the next one a year later. Gunpla also has to be patient.
I don't like this.
After a while, I heard that my friend's older brother had a computer, so I went to check it out.
Then, you can play the game by typing from the magazine. In this case, you only need to pay for the book.
When I looked it up at a bookstore, the price for PC-8001 was ridiculous.
But I really wanted a computer, so I did some research and found a computer called MSX for about 30,000 yen.
I have 15,000 yen on hand. I wanted it so badly that I finally got it after begging from my family for several months.
After that...I was able to type in BASIC from the magazine and play the game.
At that time, the instruction manual included explanations of command words and sample sources, making it possible to learn programming by self-study.
By the way, to increase speed, I had to use something called machine language, draw an assembler in my head, and type in hexadecimal numbers while looking at a HEX correspondence table.

## adolescence

A few years later, I encountered the PC-9801 at school and began programming with N88-BASIC.
Without thinking about turning this into a career, I just wanted to get hands-on with computers, so I went to an information processing school.
At this time, I learned COBOL85.
A large general-purpose machine was operating in a cold server room, and it felt like something out of a science fiction movie.
In class, we programmed each terminal and output the forms.
It's very boring when you try it.
I almost thought about quitting becoming an engineer.
Every day I do it just for the sake of my qualifications.
When it came time for my graduation thesis, I was unable to use the AS/400 and was limited to the PC-9801. BASIC was already obsolete, and it was decided to make it with Turbo C.
I enjoyed programming for the first time in a while, and secretly regained my passion to become an engineer.

## prime age

Programming became my job, and my first job was an assembler for an 8-bit microcomputer.
Until now, I had liked memory mapped I/O for external control, but I had a hard time getting used to I/O mapped I/O.
My senior colleagues handled most of the work, and I also worked on MS-C computers, but I returned to microcontrollers.
It was a relatively high-end Motorola CPU at the time, used the C language for programming, and did not have an OS installed at first, but after a while it was equipped with ITRON.
The materials were DOS Ichitaro and the IDE was Solaris.
At this time, I embedded a bug and regretted it.
Around the time I started to think that I might not be cut out to be an engineer, I started working in VB.
In Windows, functions were called by events from all over the place, and while I was keenly aware of the huge gap between the two, I created spaghetti code and added the spice of DLL hell.
While I was searching for something, I heard about object-oriented learning from a senior colleague and started studying it, but I couldn't grasp it with just documents and diagrams, so I studied MS-J++ on my own, but it was still a frustrating time.
My senior colleague had a much more difficult time than I did, and chose Delphi, an Object Pascal program, for the next step of development.
Although I struggled with class design, maintenance was completed in a surprisingly short period of time, and I realized the power of object-oriented technology.

The number of microcomputer-related projects decreased, and this time I started developing backends for Solaris.
I was supposed to access Versant DB by connecting from a terminal with CORBA, but I ended up causing trouble due to my lack of skills.
I was given the next job.
It is a system that controls measuring instruments and actuators using Windows.
Although Windows is not an RTOS, it was a system that operated 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, with an accuracy of 100ms.
I traveled to various parts of Asia and even celebrated New Year's Eve at the site, and seeing the results right before my eyes made it very rewarding.
After that, after experiencing a significant drop in orders and jobs that did not sell, I decided to move on to the next project.

It uses Windows and Oracle DB to perform monthly batches.
This went beyond the full stack, allowing us to coordinate with the source of the data and consider how to use the results.
As development progressed, the amount of data increased, and there was a lot of tuning and maintenance, but there were times when I was the only engineer on the team, so it was a struggle.
Before doing this, I was thinking about tuning Oracle easily, but this was a good opportunity to put it into practice.

## middle age

When I decided to use SAP, I was confused by the amazingness and unique philosophy of the system.
Additionally, microcontroller development involved embedded development using Debian, but this quickly came to an end.
Since then, I have expanded my skills to include C#, JavaScript, PostgreSQL, and IIS.
I think it's time for us to achieve great results.

## summary

When I think back on AS/400, I realize how amazing it is.
Although it is called legacy, it has roles from multiple terminals and performs monthly processing in an orderly manner.
If the front is the web, what you are doing is not outdated even in modern times. It is easy to understand that maintenance parts have been eliminated and the system has been virtualized.
COBOL and RPG also separate the screen and processing, which is wonderful.
However, I don't have the confidence to accept a COBOL job from now on.
Not because it's AS/400, but because it has about 30 years worth of added features.
