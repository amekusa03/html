That's right—the human ear is truly fascinating. While we don't really notice the direction (directivity) of low frequencies, we're **extremely sensitive to the direction of high frequencies**. That's why it doesn't feel out of place to tuck a subwoofer under the passenger seat or the rear seats.

However, when high frequencies are played through the coaxial speakers located near the floor by the door, the sound image inevitably gets pulled downward. No matter how much digital processing is applied via the DSP to adjust the sound, there are limits as long as the physical “output point” (the speaker’s position) remains at floor level.

If you want crisp, clear high frequencies and a sense of presence as if the singer were right in front of you—
That’s right—this is where the option of “adding a tweeter” comes into play.

## The Role of the "High-Pass Filter" in Protecting High Frequencies

Let’s take a moment here to briefly discuss a component called a tweeter.

A tweeter is, so to speak, a “tiny speaker designed to reproduce only high frequencies.”
Since both the diaphragm and the voice coil are made extremely small to reproduce delicate high frequencies, if you were to feed it the same kind of powerful bass (high power and amplitude) as you would a door speaker, the internal voice coil would burn out from the heat in no time at all.

That’s where a **“high-pass filter”**—which blocks low frequencies and allows only high frequencies to pass—becomes absolutely essential.
Basically, simply inserting a single “capacitor” in series with the tweeter is enough for it to function as a fully effective filter. If you purchase a brand-new, off-the-shelf tweeter, the appropriate capacitor is already built into the wiring (or comes with a crossover network), so for a typical DIY project, you can simply plug it in as-is without any issues.

## Bonus: A Slightly Niche Discussion on Capacitors and AC

Since we're on the subject, I'd like to share a little bit of trivia about electricity with you.

You might think, “If I want to reduce the strength of the electrical current, wouldn’t it be enough to simply insert a resistor?” However, musical signals are not direct current (DC), like that from a dry cell battery, but rather “alternating current” (AC), in which the positive and negative polarities rapidly alternate.
The reason speaker spec sheets list “4Ω” and specifically call it “impedance” rather than simply “resistance” is because, in the world of AC, the properties of resistance change depending on the frequency.

Condensers also have an interesting property: they let high frequencies (high notes) pass through easily while blocking low frequencies (low notes). By taking advantage of this property, they cut out the dangerous low frequencies that could damage the tweeter.

By the way, while electrolytic capacitors—which are commonly found on circuit boards in household appliances—have “positive and negative polarity,” the signal flowing through speakers is alternating current (AC). For this reason, it’s an ironclad rule to always use **“nonpolar”** capacitors or film capacitors in tweeter crossovers. Once you start getting into this, another deep rabbit hole begins to emerge—the idea that “the sound changes depending on the brand of capacitor”—but let’s save that discussion for another time.
