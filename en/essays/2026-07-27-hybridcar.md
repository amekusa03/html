# I've put together a summary of hybrid cars.

## 2026-07-27

Recently, when I saw the news that Toyota, Subaru, and Mazda held a joint presentation about their next-generation engine, I suddenly thought about this. Although they are all collectively referred to as "hybrid," each company actually has completely different systems.

Even if you look at a car catalog, you'll just see a bunch of mysterious abbreviations like "THS", "e-POWER", and "IMA", and to be honest, I think most people just think, "Well, it's a combination of electricity and gasoline, right?" I had a similar experience. But when you look inside, it's quite profound.

So this time, I would like to break down and introduce the three hybrid methods so that even non-engineers can understand them.

## First of all, why are there different methods?

The answer is simple: it's a question of money and division.

Creating a new system from scratch is expensive. Therefore, each manufacturer has chosen a different path depending on their field of expertise and how to utilize the parts (engines and transmissions) they have made in the past. As a result, it is divided into three methods.

## Explain the three methods as simply as possible

| Method | Typical example | Roughly speaking | What we are good at | What we are weak at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Series method | Nissan e-POWER | The engine specializes in power generation, only the motor runs | The pleasant feeling of acceleration that only a motor can provide | Efficiency decreases as the speed range increases, such as on highways |
| Parallel system | Honda IMA, European 48V mild, etc. | The engine is the main character, the motor is a helper | The existing transmission can be used as is, so it can be made cheaply | The scope for improving fuel efficiency is limited |
| Split system | Toyota THS | Automatically divides engine power into "power generation" and "driving" | High efficiency in all speed ranges and good fuel efficiency | The mechanism is complex and costly |

Try chewing on each one a little more.

### Series method = “EV loaded with a generator”

This is Nissan's e-POWER. The engine does not turn the tires at all. The system is such that the motor is solely responsible for generating electricity, and the only thing that actually makes the car run is the motor.

Therefore, the ride quality is very close to that of an EV. The smooth feeling of acceleration the moment you step on the accelerator is a weapon unique to this system. However, when cruising at a constant high speed, such as on a highway, it has the disadvantage that losses tend to occur in the conversion process from engine to power generation to motor.

### Parallel system = "Engine is the main character, motor is a helper"

This type is Honda's IMA and the 48V mild hybrid often seen in Europe. The basic relationship is that the engine runs the vehicle, and the motor provides a little help when accelerating or going up hills.

The biggest advantage of this method is that you can use the transmission you've been using as is. Since there is no need to create a new product, development costs can be reduced. It is a realistic option that is suitable when you want to achieve a moderate fuel efficiency improvement at the lowest possible price.

### Split method = "Freely distribute power generation and driving at the same time"

Toyota's THS is a typical example of this. Using a planetary gear mechanism called a "power splitting mechanism," the engine's power is automatically distributed between "for power generation" and "for turning the tires" depending on the situation.

This allows the advantages of the series system (running like a motor) and the advantages of the parallel system (high efficiency of running directly with the engine) to be used depending on the situation. It is often explained as ``there is no transmission,'' but to be precise, there is simply no physical transmission; in fact, very precise control is performed inside using a motor and gears. As a result, the structure becomes more complicated and the cost increases.

## Toyota's technology, how did Subaru cook it?

An interesting story is Subaru's new hybrid. Although Subaru uses Toyota's THS system, it stubbornly maintains its company's traditions of ``horizontally opposed engines'' and ``four-wheel drive (symmetrical AWD) that is mechanically connected to the rear wheels.''

Although THS was originally designed for horizontally mounted engines, Subaru rearranged it into a vertically mounted layout. Moreover, due to the structure of the THS, there is no "back gear", so the only way to go backwards is to rotate the motor in the opposite direction. Therefore, Subaru went so far as to significantly increase the motor's maximum torque in order to pass the harsh reverse test on hills.

Furthermore, it is interesting to note that the gasoline tank has been enlarged to promote the ability to drive over 1,000 km on a single refueling, and the finishing style is more like a Subaru that emphasizes reliability and tradition, which is different from an efficiency-oriented Toyota.

## So, in the end, which one is better?

Rather than being superior or inferior, I think the difference lies in what you prioritize.

- If you are looking for fuel efficiency and performance, use the split method (Toyota type)
- If you want to maintain brand identity and mechanical reliability → Unique interpretation like Subaru
- If you want to electrify quickly and cheaply → Parallel method

The word "hybrid" contains so many different ideas. I think the next time I look at a hybrid car catalog, I'll look at it a little differently.
