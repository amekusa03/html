# I wanted to see notifications and turn off my PC—but I got a little too greedy, so I ended up adding an “emergency stop button” to my ESP32.

2026-10-12

## background

When I'm engrossed in work at my computer, I often miss notifications that come in on my phone. But I also find it distracting to keep my phone's screen on all the time right next to my monitor. I'd like a dedicated indicator that lets me see what the notification is just by glancing at it out of the corner of my eye.

By the way, I’d like to be able to safely shut down my PC just by saying, “OK Google, turn off the PC,” to my smart speaker when I leave the room or after I get into bed. And if possible, I’d also like to adjust the volume. But to be honest, I really don’t want to do any major wiring work, like soldering a relay to the power switch terminal on the motherboard.

Just as I was harboring that rather self-serving desire, the Waveshare **ESP32-C6-GEEK** arrived.

It features a 1.14-inch color LCD (ST7789) mounted on a slim, stick-shaped device similar to a USB flash drive. It can be powered simply by plugging it directly into a USB port, and it supports the latest Wi-Fi 6 and the “Matter” smart home standard.

"If I could use just this one device to handle all the notification alerts and Matter smart home power control, wouldn't that create the ideal setup?"

I started designing with that in mind, but human desire knows no bounds. Before I knew it, a **bright red industrial “emergency stop button”** was sitting right there on my desk.

## Three Things I Wanted to Do

### 1. Consolidate smartphone and Linux notifications onto a small screen

First up is the notification feature. I consolidated notifications from my Android smartphone and Ubuntu (Linux) desktop onto the ESP32's small LCD screen.

- **Android**: Hooks into notifications using `NotificationListenerService` and renders the title, body, and time as a 240×135-pixel image. Convert it to 16-bit Little-Endian RGB565 format and send it to the ESP32 via Wi-Fi (TCP port 5555).
- **Ubuntu**: Set up a Qt daemon or Python script to monitor D-Bus (`org.freedesktop.Notifications`), and convert desktop notifications into images in the same way to send them to the ESP32.

When the ESP32 receives a notification, the LCD backlight flashes on, and the notification content is displayed. In addition, I set it up so that a high-brightness LED connected to GPIO 7 on the board flashes.

After a few seconds, the display automatically returns to the clock or normal status screen, and the LED turns off. We’ve also included a subtle design tweak—using `gpio_hold_en()` to reliably maintain the pin state—to prevent the LED from flickering or emitting leakage current during the ESP-IDF’s power-saving mode (Light Sleep).

### 2. Control Your PC from a Matter Smart Home Without Any Wires

Next up is smart home integration. I configured the ESP32-C6 as a “Dimmable Plug-in Unit” compliant with the Matter standard. From Google Home and Apple Home, it appears as if a smart plug with a dimmer is connected.

I haven't done any wiring that would require opening the PC case and tinkering with the motherboard.

- **Power On**: When a "ON" command is received from Matter, a Wake-on-LAN (WOL) magic packet is broadcast to the PC's MAC address to remotely power on the device.
- **Power OFF**: When an "OFF" command is received, an encrypted token is sent to the control daemon (port 7777) running on Ubuntu. The daemon then securely calls `sudo shutdown -h now`.
- **Volume Control**: Maps Matter brightness (LevelControl cluster: 0–254) to the PC's system volume (0–100%). If you tell your smart speaker, "Set the PC's brightness to 50%," the PC's volume will be reduced by half.

Another convenient feature was that the ESP32-C6-GEEK was connected directly to the PC’s USB port. By having the ESP32 poll the USB serial/JTAG connection status (`usb_serial_jtag_is_connected()`) every 2 seconds, it can determine with 100% accuracy whether the PC’s power is ON or OFF.

Even if you manually press the power button on your PC to turn it on or off, the ON/OFF indicator on Matter automatically syncs with the actual status.

### 3. A Man's Dream: Instantly Lock the Screen with the "Physical Emergency Stop Button"

Once I had implemented notifications and power management, my original goal should have been achieved. But as I was looking at the ESP32’s GPIO pins, I suddenly heard the devil’s whisper.

"Wouldn't it be great if there were one of those big red 'emergency stop buttons' right next to my desk?"

I connected the industrial emergency stop switch—which I couldn’t help but buy on impulse—to GPIO 8 on the ESP32. Of course, this isn’t just a toy with no real function. It’s a proper fail-safe switch (B-contact / Normally Closed).

Normally, the switch is connected to GND (LOW) internally; when the button is pressed (or in the unlikely event that the wire breaks or comes loose), the circuit opens, and the internal pull-up on the ESP32 detects a HIGH signal.

When an emergency stop is detected, an emergency token is immediately sent from the ESP32 to the Ubuntu control daemon. The daemon displays an emergency stop alert on the screen using `notify-send` and immediately issues `loginctl lock-sessions` to completely lock the desktop.

Once you're done, simply turn the mushroom-shaped button with a click to return it to its original position—thanks to this user-friendly design, the ESP32 will detect the return to the LOW state and issue an unlock command.

## The Ending: Overwhelming Satisfaction and an Unexpected Threat

I plugged the finished dongle into the back of the PC and set up the emergency stop button—which I had pulled within easy reach—in a corner of my desk.

It's more convenient than I ever imagined. I can check my notifications with just a quick glance without having to pick up my phone, and I can shut down my computer through Google Home before going to bed.

And above all, there’s nothing quite like the moment when I give the red button on my desk a sharp “smack!” whenever I have unexpected visitors or need to step away. It gives me a needlessly profound sense of satisfaction, like a pilot in a movie scene slamming down the emergency stop lever at a military base.

However, a serious security vulnerability was discovered shortly after the system went live.

When a bright red mushroom-shaped button sits on the desk, **family members who walk into the room are drawn to it and can’t help but want to press it**.

"Whoa, what's this? Can I press it?"

Before I can even finish speaking, I get smacked with a "smack!" and a beaming smile, and my PC, which I'm working on, goes black and locks up without warning—this kind of incident happens all the time.

It seems that by pursuing a fail-safe design to the extreme, we’ve ended up inviting the greatest vulnerability of all: the most unpredictable of all—human curiosity. I’m currently seriously debating whether to put a yellow “Do Not Press” warning label on the button or to cover it up.
