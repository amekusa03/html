# A 1,000-Yen Tweeter and Electrolytic Capacitors — The Car Audio Rabbit Hole: The Next Move —

2026-11-04

## background

It’s been a few days since I wrote that the high frequencies in my car weren’t coming through clearly and wondered, “I guess I’ll just have to order some new parts after all.”
Sure enough, while browsing Yahoo! Auctions, I came across a tweeter unit (Clarion SRH217) and ended up winning the bid for 1,000 yen.

No matter what condition it’s in, I’m buying it with the understanding that I’ll have to do the maintenance myself. The time spent on these experiments and maintenance is truly exciting.

## The unit that arrived and the capacitor that was hidden

The tweeter I received looks great, and you can tell the previous owner took good care of it.
I decided to start by checking the wiring.

Somewhere along the wiring, a capacitor encased in heat-shrink tubing had been installed to serve as a high-pass filter to protect the tweeter. To use as a reference for the future, I opened the tubing to check the markings, and these characters appeared.

`4.7μF 50V 105 NHG`

This is a Panasonic aluminum electrolytic capacitor (NHG series) rated for 105°C. It is a highly reliable “polarized” capacitor that is a staple in electronics projects.

## AC Signals and Polarized Capacitors

However, the music signal transmitted to car audio speakers is “alternating current (AC),” in which the positive and negative polarities constantly alternate.
Therefore, if standard polarized electrolytic capacitors are used in series as-is, they may not perform to their full potential, or the capacitors may become overloaded during prolonged use.

The previous owner probably took the time to carefully select high-quality parts from his collection and install them, matching the capacitance (μF) as needed. Seeing these traces of trial and error—a hallmark of DIY projects—is part of the charm of buying items at auction.

## Verifying Operation Using a Tester

Thinking, “Maybe there was some strain on it,” I used a digital multimeter to measure the DC resistance of the tweeter on its own before connecting it to the car.

The results were perfectly within the normal range.
The internal voice coil was in perfect working order, and the driver itself was in excellent condition. I’m really grateful to have found a driver in such good condition for just 1,000 yen.

## Countermeasures and New System Plans

From here on, we'll optimize this unit to suit your environment.

First, I removed the existing capacitors and replaced them with “Nichicon Metallized Film Capacitors (2.2 μF, 100 V),” which are safe for use with AC signals and offer better sound quality. I was able to purchase these at another reasonable price—250 yen for a set of five.

I’m also planning a slightly creative setup for the installation.
The approach involves leaving the existing wiring in the front doors completely untouched and **“removing the rear speakers and repurposing the wiring for those rear channels for the tweeters.”**

This way, the job is done simply by tapping into the wiring with a custom extension cable—equipped with a capacitor—using spade terminals, right next to the DSP (PZ-C7) I installed earlier.
Furthermore, using the DSP’s Fader (front-rear balance) function offers the significant advantage of being able to independently fine-tune the tweeter volume right from the driver’s seat.

I plan to build my own in-line network wiring using a soldering iron and heat-shrink tubing, and as soon as I have all the parts, I’ll tackle the assembly and test the sound.
It looks like the rabbit hole of car audio is only going to get deeper.
