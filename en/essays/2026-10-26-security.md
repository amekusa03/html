# The Pitfalls Behind Convenience: The Risks of Single Sign-On and VPNs

## 2026-10-26 / IT

When it comes to security, we tend to assume that “the company will take care of it.” However, there are actually quite a few things we can do as individuals. Today, I’d like us to think about that together.

It's a hassle to enter your password over and over again.
"I want to be able to securely connect to our internal systems with just one click, even when I'm outside the office."

When using IT systems, everyone naturally wants a “more convenient and hassle-free solution.” Prime examples of this are **single sign-on (SSO) and VPN (virtual private network)**.

Since both are often presented as “technologies that enhance security,” many people mistakenly believe that “as long as they use these, they’ll be safe.”

However, from a practical perspective, convenience goes hand in hand with the risk that even a single misstep could lead to total failure. In this article, I’ll explain—while avoiding technical jargon as much as possible—the dangers lurking within these two systems and the essential measures needed to prevent them.

The Master Key That Opens Every Door: The Dangers of SSO
SSO is a system that allows you to access all connected apps and cloud services with a single login.

To put it another way, it’s like having a master key that opens everything—the front door of your home, your safe, your car, and your office. Since you don’t have to carry around multiple keys, it’s very convenient in everyday life.

So, what would happen if you dropped that master key on the street or if someone stole it? Thieves would be able to open not only your front door, but also your safe, your car, and your office.

The same applies to SSO environments secured solely by a username and password. If just one password is compromised, all the critical information you have access to could be hijacked at once. This structural weakness—the so-called “single point of failure,” where a breach in one place spells disaster—is the biggest pitfall of SSO.

A Castle Where No One Suspects a Thief Who Has Passed Through the Checkpoint: The Dangers of VPNs
With the rise of remote work, VPNs have become a familiar sight. This technology allows users to connect from a computer outside the company’s network via the Internet, as if they were on the internal LAN.

Traditional VPNs are based on the concept of a castle with walls and a large gate—much like a checkpoint. The idea is that the world outside the gate is dangerous, while the world inside is safe and reserved for friends and allies. If you give the gatekeeper the correct password (username and password), they will open the gate and let you in.

However, there are three serious blind spots here.

If your password is stolen, it’s like a thief walking right in through the front door. By using phishing scams or leaked passwords, attackers can gain access while posing as legitimate users.
Once they’re inside, no one suspects a thing. They’re treated as “friends who came through the gate,” and are granted free access to servers and shared folders.
The vulnerability lies in the gatekeeper itself. Incidents where flaws (vulnerabilities) are found in VPN software, allowing attackers to slip through the gate without a password, are all too common.
Rather than thinking, “I’m safe because I’m connected via VPN,” you should maintain a sense of urgency, realizing that “if the VPN is breached, the network behind it will be left completely unprotected.”

The Key to Security: Why MFA?
Whether it’s the loss of a master key or unauthorized access via a fake passphrase, **MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication)** prevents these threats at their root.

MFA is a system that requires you to combine at least two of the following three factors when logging in.

What you know: Password, PIN
What you have: Authentication app on your smartphone, one-time code sent via SMS, physical security key
Who you are: Fingerprint, facial recognition
Let’s say a thief steals your password. With a system based solely on “username and password,” you would grant them access at that point. However, with MFA, immediately after entering your password, you’ll be prompted to “enter the 6-digit code sent to your smartphone.” Since the thief doesn’t have your smartphone, they won’t be able to proceed any further.

What's more, you'll receive a notification on your smartphone saying, "A login attempt was made." This means you can immediately realize that your password has been compromised.

Combine this smartphone or biometric authentication with both the SSO master key and the VPN gatekeeper. Just doing that is enough to prevent the worst-case scenario—where a password leak leads to total disaster.

Summary: Behind “convenience” lies the need for appropriate security measures.
A system that anyone can use easily, anytime and anywhere, is certainly appealing.

However, when it comes to security, “simple and convenient” refers to a situation where the system takes over the security management responsibilities that should rightfully fall on the user. If there aren’t safeguards in place—such as MFA or strict access controls—behind the scenes, it’s as if you’re simply enjoying the convenience while leaving the door wide open.

SSO and VPN systems that rely solely on a password are no different from an unlocked door. If your web services, cloud platforms, or internal tools allow you to configure it, enable MFA right away.

Don't be afraid to take a few seconds to do this. It's the surest way to protect both yourself and your company's assets.
