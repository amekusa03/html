# Can You Program an ESP32 Without a PC? — On-Site Maintenance Using Just an Android Smartphone and OTG —

2026-11-22

## A "Blind Spot" I Discovered After Getting a Laptop

When working on microcontroller projects or operating IoT devices, there are inevitably times when you’ll want to make a quick on-site adjustment to the program running on an installed ESP32.

Until now, I’d convinced myself that every time this happened, I had no choice but to drag myself out of bed and grab my laptop. Of course, wireless updates are possible if OTA (Over-The-Air) functionality is built into the firmware via Wi-Fi in advance, but for devices that operate standalone—such as in-car systems or sensors on the balcony—or in areas with poor signal reception, connecting a physical USB cable is the most reliable and foolproof method.

Just then, it suddenly occurred to me, “Can’t I program the ESP32 directly from my Android phone?” So I looked into it and discovered that **you can actually flash the device using just an Android phone and a USB cable**.

As someone who had bought a used laptop to use for writing on the go and had been racking my brain over how to set it up, this was a perfect example of “the answer being right under my nose”—it’s the kind of convenience I wish I’d known about sooner.

## A clear division of roles: development on the main computer, fieldwork on the smartphone

When you break down how it works, it’s actually quite logical.

I quickly handle the heavy build tasks—such as compiling and linking—on my desktop PC (my main machine), which I’m used to working on, and then transfer only the generated binary (the `.bin` file) to my smartphone. Then, using the smartphone’s USB OTG (host mode) and a short USB Type-C cable, simply connect it directly to the ESP32 and flash the firmware.

When I searched Google Play, I found that there were already several apps specifically designed for this purpose.

* **ESPFlash**: A go-to app that not only writes to the flash via USB OTG but also handles flash dumps (extraction) and serial monitor display. However, the free version displays ads, which can be a bit distracting when using the app in the field.
* **ESP32_Flasher**: A flasher app from the same series with a simple design that doesn't display ads. It's easy to use and minimizes the risk of accidental taps when you need to work quickly on-site, such as outdoors or inside a car.

Both apps share the same basic concept as writing tools on a PC (such as `esptool.py`). Simply specify the `.bin` file on the device, enter the **offset**—which is the starting address for writing—and press the execute button; a progress bar will then appear and the writing process will be completed.

## 3 Practical Tips for Avoiding Mistakes on the Job

Now that we understand the theory, I’ve summarized the key points for ensuring the workflow—“build on the host computer → upload to the smartphone at the site”—runs smoothly without any issues.

### 1. Decide on the “standard” for binary files and offsets in your development environment
Since smartphones do not have a build system, you must manually specify the memory address (offset) where the code will be written in the app. For example, in standard application areas such as the Arduino environment or ESP-IDF, it is standard practice to specify `0x10000`.

At first, I was concerned that “checking the address every time I write data would be a hassle,” but as long as you’ve settled on the framework and partition configuration you normally use, the values you specify will always be the same. If you jot down the addresses for each development environment (e.g., `0x10000` for the app area, `0x1000` for the bootloader, etc.) in a text editor, you’ll almost never get confused when working on-site.

### 2. Avoid accessing files directly from the cloud; save them to your phone's internal storage instead.
The easiest way to transfer a `.bin` file from your main PC to your smartphone is to use cloud storage services such as Google Drive or OneDrive.

However, if you try to access files in the cloud directly from the Flasher app, there is a risk that the files may not load properly due to the behavior of the file picker or may be affected by network conditions.
“After uploading to the cloud, first explicitly download and save the file to the `Download` folder on your smartphone.” Just by adding this extra step, you can reliably write data offline even when working outdoors or inside a car where there is no cellular signal.

### 3. Be mindful of USB OTG compatibility and power drops
Just as when programming on a PC, if you attempt to flash the ESP32 while it has numerous external sensors, relays, LEDs, and other components connected to it, the power supply from your smartphone (5V bus power) may not be sufficient, and a voltage drop could cause a programming error. To be on the safe side, it’s best to disconnect the external circuitry or ensure a sufficient power supply during programming.

In addition, the stability of device recognition can vary depending on the compatibility between the smartphone’s OTG controller and the USB-to-serial conversion chip on the ESP32 board (such as the CP2102, CH340, or the ESP32-S3’s built-in USB CDC), as well as the quality of the OTG adapter and cable used. It’s a golden rule to perform a connection test at home before heading out to the site.

## Convenience Right in Your Pocket

You no longer have to lug around a heavy laptop and set up your keyboard in cramped spaces or under the hood. All you need is the smartphone in your pocket and a single short cable, and you can complete the firmware update right on the spot.

"Take your time developing on your main computer's desktop, then quickly handle on-site maintenance using your smartphone." With this streamlined approach, DIY projects involving in-vehicle microcontrollers and outdoor IoT devices are likely to become even more accessible and enjoyable.
