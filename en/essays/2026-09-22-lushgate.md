# Automatic Irrigation System for Farm Fields: A Return from the “Big Guns on Big Ships” Approach — A Design That Starts with a Solar Generator and Ends with “100-Yen Store Tupperware and Dry Cell Batteries”

2026-09-22 / ESP32 / IoT

When I decided to build “LushGate,” a device that automatically waters fields, I already had a grand blueprint unfolding in my mind.

A homemade 10W solar panel. A massive lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) battery (12V, 6Ah / 72Wh). An MPPT charge controller and a step-down DC-DC converter. My “home power plant,” proudly sitting in a corner of the field, and the sturdy wiring extending from it.

"With this, it'll run semi-permanently, completely off-grid, without being affected by the weather."

Every boy has dreamed of the "big gun" philosophy at least once. But the god of electronics always confronts you with cold, hard reality the moment you start tapping away on your calculator.

## A Rigorous Calculation of Power Consumption

First, I tried to carefully calculate the power consumption of the “ESP32-C3” microcontroller, which handles the control functions.

There’s no need to monitor a plant’s watering status hundreds of times per second. It operates in an intermittent mode: it wakes up from Deep Sleep once every 3 minutes, sends a current through the rain sensor for just a few milliseconds to measure rainfall, and then falls back into Deep Sleep. The standby current during Deep Sleep is approximately 0.1 mA, and the current consumed during the wake-up and measurement (5 ms) every 3 minutes is virtually negligible. A single 10-minute watering session per day (when the relay is activated) consumes about 60 mA. When you add up these figures for a full 24-hour period, the total daily current consumption amounts to a mere **30–40 mAh (approximately 0.15 Wh)**.

At that moment, I stopped what I was doing and stared intently at the 72Wh LiFePO4 battery right in front of me.

“...Even if we buried this in complete darkness where the sun never shines, wouldn’t it keep running for **more than a year**?”

As for the 10W solar panel, just half a day of sunlight was enough to instantly charge enough power to last several months. For what was essentially a small garden sprinkler, I had set up a massive power source capable of continuously powering streetlights and security cameras. It was an overwhelmingly—and devastatingly—excessive specification.

## The Demon of Motor Noise and the Temptation of "Physical Separation"

Another challenge was addressing the noise from the watering pump (a battery-powered kerosene pump).

Inside the kerosene pump is a very ordinary DC motor with brushes. The sparks it emits when it rotates, the back-EMF, and the intense brush noise are the natural enemies of delicate microcontrollers.

A while back, when I tried to power both an ESP32 and a motor simultaneously using a step-down DC-DC converter connected to the same power line, I experienced an “endless reboot hell” where the ESP32 would brownout and repeatedly reset the moment the motor started spinning. Whether I added a bypass capacitor or built a snubber circuit, living in constant fear of noise interference was not good for my mental health.

The conclusion reached regarding the circuit design was **"complete power supply independence (physical isolation)"**.

The pump was originally designed to run on “two AA batteries (3V).” In that case, we can simply leave the pump’s power supply as is, using AA batteries. On the ESP32 side, we just need to close the switch contacts of a 3V relay (JQC-3F) with a click. There isn’t even a single GND wire connecting the microcontroller side to the motor side. By completely isolating them electrically, no matter how much noise the motor generates, it cannot reach the ESP32. This is the ultimate and most reliable noise countermeasure.

## And on to “All Dry Cell Batteries”

The moment I separated the power supplies for the control system and the drive system, my final—and greatest—doubt reared its head.

“...Then, wouldn’t we also be able to do without the solar power supply on the ESP32 side?”

The ESP32-C3 consumes only 30 to 40 mAh per day. Three standard AA alkaline batteries (4.5 V) have a capacity of approximately 2,000 mAh. If you supply 4.5V to the USB port on the ESP32 development board, a stable 3.3V output is generated via the regulator on the board. Based on this calculation, **the board should easily continue operating for 2 to 3 months on just three AA batteries**.

When planting vegetable seedlings in the spring, insert a brand-new dry-cell battery. After that, no maintenance is required until summer passes and the fall harvest arrives.

There’s no need to build a mounting frame for the solar panels, nor is there any need to wipe dirt off them. There’s absolutely no worry about the panels being blown away by typhoon winds, nor about tripping over long cables running across the raised beds in the garden.

## The Aesthetics of Subtraction: A Palm-Sized Conclusion

As a result, these are the parts I ended up with.

- **Microcontroller System**: 3 AA batteries ── (USB) ──> ESP32-C3 + Rain Sensor + 3V Relay
- **Pump Drive System**: 2 single batteries ── (relay contacts) ──> kerosene pump

All of this is supposed to fit into a single airtight Tupperware container.

Tie a Tupperware container to a stake in the garden, attach the rain sensor board facing upward with double-sided tape, and insert the pump hose into a water jug. That’s all it takes to create a fully autonomous IoT sprinkler system that intelligently skips watering on rainy days and waters thoroughly only during prolonged dry spells.

I tend to envision sophisticated, large-scale systems, but I was reminded once again that what really works on the ground is “minimal simplicity”—a system that has been thoroughly stripped of all waste.
