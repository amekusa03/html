# A story about touching the ESP32's "interrupt" with bare hands

![alt text](essays/2026-08-05-esp32.png)

Last week, I suddenly found myself wondering, "ESP32, what happens inside that tiny chip when you press just one button?"

Questions like this are usually forgotten after a while, but this time I didn't forget them. Instead, I woke up in the middle of the night and started opening the data sheet on my smartphone, thinking, ``What is an interrupt matrix?'' and I thought I was doing something wrong. I'm going to go out of my way to dig down to the register level, even though you can just write attachInterrupt() in one line in the Arduino IDE.

However, the functions provided by ESP-IDF and Arduino are essentially "magic wands." If you shake it, it moves. It's convenient, but the cane itself doesn't tell you why it moves. This time, I recorded a peek inside the cane.

## First of all, who said “interrupt”?

The first thing that surprised me was that there were far more people telling the CPU to "interrupt processing" than I expected.

Broadly speaking, there are two types. One is the "inside" guys. It is directly connected to the CPU core, and has three timers, two software interrupts, and a performance monitor. Since he is a relative, he reacts quickly.

The other group is the ``outside'' people, and there are a lot more of them. UART, GPIO, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, SPI, I2C... I counted 71 of them. It's a little funny to imagine 71 people gathered around a CPU saying, ``Listen to me.'' I wish the morning meetings at my workplace were this noisy, but when they actually are, it's usually a problem.

## A game where 71 people compete for 26 chairs.

Now, with these 71 external forces, there are only 32 entrances for each core that the CPU can accept. Moreover, 6 of them are reserved for internal use only, so there are actually 26 chairs for outsiders to sit on.

26 chairs for 71 people. It's not a fuss about Fruits Basket.

This is decided by a traffic controller called the "Interruption Matrix," and by manipulating registers like PRO_X_MAP, developers can freely decide which peripherals should sit in which chairs. This seems to be the reason why certain functions are not tied to specific pins, and after learning this, I thought to myself, ``I see, so that's why there was some strange flexibility in the pin arrangement.''

However, there is one strict rule. When multiple sources are seated together in one chair, the interrupt must be a "level trigger." This is because if you use edge triggers, there is a risk that the ISR may miss a signal coming from another source while it is processing it.

This is similar to the difference between a method that makes a doorbell ring for just a moment (Edge) and a method that continues to ring as long as you press the doorbell (Level). If you share a doorbell that only rings momentarily with multiple people, you will normally miss the part that rings while someone else is answering the door. If you keep pressing it, you will notice that it will still beeping even after the response is finished. It's obvious when you say it's obvious, but once you get hooked on implementing it, you forget this obvious thing.

## Even if you can sit on a chair, there is a waiting list.

Even if you can safely sit on one of the 26 seats, that doesn't mean you'll be taken care of right away. This time, a hierarchy called "priority level" is waiting.

The lowest level 1 is "UserException", which can be written normally in C language. Intermediate levels 2 and 3 are also in C language, and this is about all that can be handled by ESP-IDF. At levels 4 and 5, the hurdles suddenly rise, and it becomes necessary to write directly in assembly without going through C stack operations. and apex level 7, xt_nmi. Non-maskable interrupts, or interrupts that "cannot be denied," also require assembly.

What's interesting is that at the moment a high-priority process pushes aside a low-priority process and interrupts, the CPU properly saves the state of the work it was currently doing before moving on. The Xtensa instruction set has a unique mechanism called ``windowing ABI,'' which includes a process called ``register spilling,'' which spills the contents of registers onto the stack.

I think this is very similar to when you get an unexpected phone call during a meeting, and you write down the length of the conversation in your memo before answering the phone. If you don't write it down, after the phone call ends, you'll be left wondering, ``Oh, what was I talking about earlier?'' The same goes for the CPU, which dutifully writes notes in order to accurately resume the original process when it returns.

## Only one register determines jump destination

Once you have decided on your priorities, all you have to do is decide where to fly. This was controlled by a special register called VECBASE.

The CPU calculates the jump destination by adding a "fixed offset" determined for each type of exception to this VECBASE address set by the wsr instruction. It jumps to a predetermined address, such as VECBASE+0x000 for a window overflow, VECBASE+0x180 for a level 2 interrupt, and so on.

Although the address is fixed, the contents (the contents of the ISR) can be written freely by the developer. This is what's interesting about bare metal, and I think it's like, ``The neighborhood association decides where the mailbox is, but it's up to you to decide what to put inside.''

And here I learned the most important point. The ISR must be placed in internal RAM (IRAM). The reason is that the cache stops while writing to Flash. If the ISR were to rely on Flash code, the code would become unreadable at that exact moment and the entire system would be blown away. This is the true nature of a problem called "Cache Error," which sounds unassuming at first glance, but can actually be fatal.

## What I learned after going around

Starting with 71 signal sources, there is a scramble for 26 chairs, a hierarchy of priorities, a sweep of registers, and finally a VECBASE to land at the correct address. After following this series of steps, I finally realized how many procedures the attachInterrupt() line was responsible for behind the scenes.

Even if you don't know how this works, the code will work fine. It works, but when I encounter a phenomenon like ``for some reason the CPU stalls'' or ``for some reason it crashes only while writing Flash'', I feel like the response speed will be completely different depending on whether I have this map or not. There's a difference between someone who only knows how to swing a cane and someone who knows the wiring inside the cane.

Next, I'm thinking of actually opening xtensa_vectors.S and comparing the map I just assembled in my head with the real source code. I feel like I'm going to wake up again at a strange time in the middle of the night.
