# Wirelessly Connecting a Balcony Environmental Sensor: Outdoor Measurements Using ESP-NOW and Battery Power

2026-11-20

## How to Retrieve Data from Outside the Window

As you move toward automated air conditioning control and smart home integration within your home, you’ll inevitably find yourself wanting “outdoor environmental data.”

In particular, if you can monitor the amount of sunlight (illuminance) on the balcony and the outdoor temperature in real time, you can achieve a higher level of automation—such as “closing the curtains when the sun gets stronger” or “turning on the exhaust fan when the outside air gets cooler.”

However, when attempting to measure the outdoor environment from inside a room, it is impossible to obtain accurate readings due to the thermal insulation of the window glass, as well as the effects of solar control film and reflection. On the other hand, running a flat cable through the gaps in the window frame not only looks unsightly but also carries the risk of rainwater intrusion and cable breakage.

“In that case, why not just place a small, battery-powered satellite unit on the balcony and have it transmit data wirelessly to the main unit inside?”—With that thought in mind, I launched a project to develop a standalone outdoor environmental sensor unit.

## Reasons to Choose “ESP-NOW” Over Wi-Fi

When building battery-powered IoT devices, the biggest challenge is the power consumption of wireless communication.

A typical Wi-Fi connection takes anywhere from a few seconds to over ten seconds after waking from sleep to scan for access points, perform an encryption handshake, and obtain an IP address via DHCP. During that time, the microcontroller continues to consume a current of around 80 to 100 mA, so when powered by dry-cell batteries, the battery drains in no time.

That's why they chose Espressif's proprietary wireless communication protocol, **ESP-NOW**.

ESP-NOW uses the 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi physical layer but does not follow the standard network connection sequence at all. It sends packets directly to the MAC address of a pre-registered recipient, receives an immediate hardware ACK, and terminates the process. The entire process takes only **10 to 20 milliseconds (0.01 to 0.02 seconds)**.

If it can be done in such a short time, even though it draws a small amount of current for just a split second during transmission, the microcontroller can be kept in deep sleep mode (about 40 µA) for most of the time. If measurements are transmitted once every five minutes, battery life can be significantly extended.

## How to Adapt to Router Channel Changes

However, this requires a bit of technical ingenuity.

The indoor base unit (ESP32-C3) is constantly connected (in STA mode) to the home Wi-Fi router for Google Home and Matter integration, as well as for the web dashboard. The Wi-Fi router may automatically switch the channel it uses (channels 1–13) depending on when it starts up or the level of network congestion.

The ESP-NOW won't receive a signal unless it transmits on the same channel as the parent unit. However, if the child unit were to perform a brute-force scan of channels 1 through 13 every time, the boot time would increase, negating the benefits of power savings.

That’s when we came up with the idea of a “channel auto-tracking and failover” mechanism using **RTC Fast Memory**.

1. **Channel Retention**:
   Store it as `RTC_DATA_ATTR uint8_t s_cached_channel = 1;` in the subunit. Since power is maintained to the RTC memory even during deep sleep, the previous channel number remains unchanged.
2. **Fastest Transmission Under Normal Conditions (99.9%)**:
   Upon waking up, it transmits to the parent unit using the cached channel. If a hardware ACK (`ESP_NOW_SEND_SUCCESS`) is returned from the parent unit, the task is completed in just 10 to 20 ms, and it immediately enters sleep mode.
3. **During a failover (when the router changes channels)**:
   Only if no ACK is received (when the router switches channels) will the device sequentially send packets on channels 1 through 13 to search for the parent unit. The channel on which an ACK was successfully received is saved by overwriting the existing `s_cached_channel` value, and that channel number is used for subsequent transmissions.

If you set this up, it won't waste power under normal conditions, and even in the unlikely event of a router reboot or channel change, it will automatically maintain its connection to the main unit without losing it.

We also consolidated the packets to be sent and received into an extremely small data structure of just 12 bytes, as shown below.

```c
typedef struct __attribute__((packed)) {
    float lux;              // ベランダ照度 (Lux, 0.0 〜 100000.0)
    float temp_celsius;     // ベランダ外気温 (摂氏 ℃, -20.0 〜 80.0)
    float battery_voltage;  // 子機の電池電圧 (V, 例: 2.95)
} esp_now_sensor_packet_t;
```

## Hardware Selection and Division of Roles

The microcontroller in the slave unit uses the **Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32C3** (or SuperMini), which features a low-quiescent-current regulator that keeps the deep-sleep current down to approximately 40 µA. Since it does not include a USB-to-serial conversion IC, it is reassuring that unnecessary standby current can be reduced to the absolute minimum.

The sensor is equipped with a CdS cell capable of detecting light ranging from strong direct sunlight to the dim light of dusk, as well as a B3950 thermistor for measuring outdoor temperature. Additionally, a voltage-divider resistor was installed to detect the voltage drop across the dry cell battery, and the system was designed to transmit the remaining battery level from the slave unit to the master unit.

A key feature is that a thermistor is also directly connected to the indoor base unit. By not only receiving data from outside but also measuring the indoor temperature itself, the base unit can independently compare and manage the temperatures both “inside the room” and “outside on the balcony.”

## Group Devices on Google Home Using Matter over Wi-Fi

The data collected by the parent unit is synced to Google Home using **Matter over Wi-Fi**.

The parent unit acts as a Matter server and hosts and exposes the following three endpoints within a single device (multi-endpoint configuration).

- **Endpoint 1 (Light Sensor)**: Balcony illuminance (received from the sub-unit)
- **Endpoint 2 (Temperature Sensor)**: Indoor temperature (thermistor directly connected to the parent unit)
- **Endpoint 3 (Temperature Sensor)**: Outdoor temperature on the balcony (received from the sub-unit)

When you open the Google Home app, the "living room temperature," "outdoor temperature on the balcony," and "brightness on the balcony" from a single microcontroller are neatly arranged as separate tiles.

Since synchronization is handled via local Matter communication rather than relying on the cloud, response times are extremely fast. The web dashboard also allows you to see at a glance the temperature difference between indoors and outdoors, as well as the remaining battery levels of the child units.

## Can it last two years on just two AA batteries?

Finally, let's estimate the battery life of the handset, which is something I'm curious about.

- **Sleep Current**: Approx. 40 µA
- **Transmission Current**: Approximately 100 mA on average (takes only about 0.02 seconds)
- **Measurement Frequency**: Once every 5 minutes (288 times per day)

Based on this, the daily electricity consumption can be calculated as follows.

$$\text{Daily power consumption} \approx (40\,\mu\text{A} \times 24\,\text{h}) + \left(100\,\text{mA} \times \frac{0.02\,\text{s}}{3600\,\text{s}} \times 288\right) \approx 0.96\,\text{mAh} + 0.16\,\text{mAh} = 1.12\,\text{mAh/day}$$

If we conservatively estimate the effective capacity of two AA alkaline batteries (3.0 V in series) to be approximately 800–900 mAh:

$$\frac{800\,\text{mAh}}{1.12\,\text{mAh/day}} \approx 714\,\text{days} \quad (\mathbf{approximately\,1.9\,years})$$

Even taking into account the self-discharge of dry-cell batteries and the decrease in capacity due to freezing temperatures in the dead of winter, it appears the device can be left on the balcony **for one and a half to nearly two years without any maintenance**. If all it takes is replacing the batteries once every two years, that’s more than enough for practical purposes.
