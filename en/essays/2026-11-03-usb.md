# A Glowing LED, a Silent PC: The Pitfalls of the ESP32-S3 and USB Cable

2026-11-03

## background

The ESP32 is palm-sized, features both Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and even supports native USB.
Recently, ultra-compact boards like the ESP32-S3 Super Mini and C3 Super Mini have become readily available at affordable prices, making them the perfect companions for writing code and experimenting.

I’d been tinkering with the ESP32-C3 until just the other day, but I decided to try running the ESP32-S3 this time.
I immediately plugged the Type-C cable from my PC into the S3.
The blue LED on the board started flashing energetically.

……All right, the power connection is fine. Let's upload the program.
I sat down at my PC, full of enthusiasm, but for some reason, the development environment wouldn't recognize the port.

## The Silent PC

Open the console and try typing the familiar command.

```bash
$ lsusb
Bus 001 Device 001: ID 1d6b:0002 Linux Foundation 2.0 root hub
Bus 001 Device 002: ID 0409:0059 NEC Corp. HighSpeed Hub
Bus 001 Device 003: ID 1ea7:0064 SHARKOON Technologies GmbH 2.4GHz Wireless mouse
Bus 001 Device 004: ID 045e:0737 Microsoft Corp. Optical Mouse
```

All I can see are the mouse and the hub. There’s no sign of the ESP32-S3 anywhere.
Even when I plug and unplug the cables while looking at `dmesg -w`, not a single log entry appears.
It’s complete silence—not even an error log appears.

"I was just using this cable to program the ESP32-C3, so there's no way it could be broken."

That conviction completely clouded his judgment.

## Wrong Conclusions and a Tough Struggle

The first thing I suspected was the ESP32’s signature boot mode.
“Maybe the firmware is corrupted and the USB is dead,” I thought, as I pressed the BOOT button—smaller than a grain of rice—with the tip of a pair of tweezers while plugging it back in. But there was no response.

Next, I suspected the Linux OS environment.
“Is it a lack of permissions for Ubuntu’s udev rules?” “Is it something to do with the kernel’s CDC-ACM driver?” I went through the settings again and again, checking every possibility.
But since the kernel wasn’t even detecting the physical connection events of the USB device in the first place, there was no point in tinkering with the software or permissions.

Right in front of me, only the blue LED on the circuit board continues to glow steadfastly as always.
I’m completely at a loss. It’s exactly like that nightmare all over again—the “night the brand-new ESP32 fell silent.”

## The Decisive Blow

I decided to set aside the assumption that “since it worked on another board, the cable must be fine.”
Just to be safe, I’ll try connecting my smartphone to the PC using that cable.

The message "Charging" appeared on the smartphone screen.
However, even when I called `lsusb` on the PC, the number of devices still didn't increase.

That's when I finally figured out what was going on.
**"This cable only has the power lines working (the data lines aren't connected)……!"**

## Why did “C3 work, but S3 didn’t”?

Questions remain. So why was communication working fine on the ESP32-C3 just a moment ago?
As I investigated further, I began to see the physical limitations unique to these low-cost, ultra-compact boards.

1. **Tolerances (Play) in Low-Cost Type-C Connectors**
   Low-cost boards like the Super Mini have loose dimensional tolerances for their connectors. It appears that subtle differences in the length of the male connector on the cable or the thickness of its shell caused a physical contact issue: “While the connector on the C3 reached all the way in and made contact with the data pins, the connector on the S3 fell slightly short and only made contact with the power pins.”
2. **Type-C Single-Sided Wiring Trap**
   Some inexpensive cables and circuit boards are wired with data pins (D+/D-) on only one side. The phenomenon where communication works or doesn't work depending on which side is facing up is a common occurrence in this field.
3. **The Sensitivity of the Built-in USB Controller**
   Compared to dedicated serial conversion chips (such as the CP2102 or CH340), the ESP32-S3’s built-in USB PHY is sensitive to signal quality. If a low-quality cable is used, the signal may be overwhelmed by noise, causing the handshake to fail.

## solution

I pulled the sturdy Type-C data cable that came with my smartphone out from the back of the drawer and reconnected it.

```bash
$ lsusb
Bus 001 Device 014: ID 303a:1001 Espressif USB JTAG/serial debug unit
```

I figured it out right away.
A port appeared on `/dev/ttyACM0`, and the program started writing to it as if nothing had happened.
What on earth was all that struggle over the past few hours all about?

## Lesson

- **If there is absolutely no response from **`dmesg`**, you should 100% suspect a problem with the physical layer (cables, connections, or power supply).**
- **The biggest trap is letting your guard down, thinking, “This cable worked on another device just a moment ago.”**
- **If you suspect a problem, try flipping the connector 180 degrees or switching to a different, thicker communication cable.**

Before suspecting software bugs or kernel settings, you should first check the basic “physical” aspects.
