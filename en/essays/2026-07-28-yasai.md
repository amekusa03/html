# Apparently the word "pesticide-free" is actually banned.

If you see a pop-up that says ``Pesticide-Free Vegetables'' at a supermarket, it's probably an older store. I was surprised to learn such a story. When I looked into it, I found that the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries had banned the labeling itself as ``pesticide-free'' as of 2007. The official term now is ``no use of pesticides during the cultivation period.'' It's a rather harsh word, but once you know the reason, it makes sense. It is impossible to completely prove that there are no pesticides left in the soil or that no pesticides have flown in from neighboring fields, and the word "pesticide-free" gives consumers too much hope. The government is also very disciplined in its approach to words.

## “Organic” and “pesticide-free” are two different things.

First of all, I had to correct myself because I thought "organic" and "pesticide-free" meant the same thing.

"Organic" is a legal term based on the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries' organic JAS standards. Without third-party certification, you cannot even write "organic" or "organic" on the package. The soil must not have been chemically fertilized for at least two years before sowing or planting (three years for perennial crops), and genetically modified technology is prohibited in principle. It's a pretty serious system.

On the other hand, "Pesticide-free (no pesticides used during the cultivation period)" is a label that specifies that no pesticides are used during the cultivation period, and the restrictions on fertilizers are not as strict as organic JAS. Although they may seem similar, they are actually two words based on different rules.

## “Organic = 100% pesticide-free” does not mean

What is even more confusing is the fact that, even if organic JAS certification has been obtained, the use of 39 types of naturally derived pesticides is exceptionally permitted in emergencies where serious damage to agricultural products is likely to occur. In other words, even if it has an "organic" sign, it does not guarantee that it is free of pesticides.

The same goes for seeds; originally, seeds that comply with organic JAS standards should be used, but if they are difficult to obtain, it is permitted to use regular seeds or seedlings. Therefore, there are actually not many vegetables that are 100% organic from seed. The system is more flexible than I expected, or rather, it is closer to reality.

It seems that this certification is quite difficult to obtain. Strict field management, detailed records, on-site surveys, certification fees... Because of this heavy burden, some small-scale farmers and elderly farmers are unable to obtain certification even though they are essentially practicing organic farming. It is important to remember that the presence or absence of a label does not necessarily correspond to the actual farming method.

## So, is it good for your body?

In terms of safety, it is said that pesticide residues in vegetables distributed in Japan do not have an immediate effect on health as long as they are below standard values. According to a study by the Nagano Prefecture Environmental Conservation Research Institute, the estimated intake amount is only 0.01% to 1.30% of the Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI). Looking at the numbers alone, it seems like there's no need to be overly nervous in the first place.

However, research has shown that children who regularly eat organic produce have lower levels of metabolites of organophosphate pesticides in their urine than children who do not. In particular, choosing organic vegetables seems to be one way to reduce the risk of neonicotinoid pesticides, which are said to be difficult to remove with water.

In terms of nutritional value, it has been reported that organic vegetables tend to contain more flavonoid antioxidants, and spinach, tomatoes, and potatoes have 20-40% more vitamins and minerals. Regarding sugar content and flavor, some studies have found that ``organic is richer,'' while others have found ``no significant difference,'' so honestly, it probably depends on the product and the cultivation environment.

What was surprising was the risks specific to grains. If pesticides are not used on crops such as wheat, Fusarium head blight will not be adequately controlled, and the risk of mycotoxin outbreaks may actually increase. I was told that if you choose pesticide-free products, you will need to undergo a self-inspection to ensure safety before shipping, which makes sense to me.

## Let's talk about price

And what I'm concerned about is the price. According to data from 2018, vegetables with the organic JAS mark are 1.5 to 1.8 times more expensive than standard domestic products. 155% for radish, 174% for carrot, 181% for onion, and 187% for green pepper. It's almost double.

What's interesting is that there is a clear difference in enthusiasm between producers and consumers. Approximately 65% ​​of producers are satisfied with the current selling price, while more than half of distributors and consumers take the stance that they will pay if it is 10% higher, and less than 10% would accept a 40% to 50% higher price. The perception of a "reasonable price" between those who put in the effort and those who spend their wallets is more misaligned than you might think.

## In the end, what should you look at and choose?

After researching so far, I found that the points to consider when choosing one are surprisingly simple.

* Check the production history (traceability) of who made it, where it was made, and how it was made, using the QR code on the package.
* If you want legal support, the presence or absence of the organic JAS mark should be your top priority.
* Trust retailers and farmers who have their own safety standards.
* Understand that the irregular appearance or moth-eaten appearance is the result of efforts to reduce environmental impact.
* In the end, check the taste and aroma with your own tongue and choose one that you are satisfied with.

The simple formula that ``organic = pesticide-free = absolutely safe'' does not seem to hold true. There are exceptions to the system, there are gaps in prices between production and consumption, and there are variations in nutritional value. Still, the direction of reducing the risk of pesticide residues and reducing the burden on the environment makes sense. Rather than just reading the label at face value, make your choice after understanding the mechanism behind the label. Maybe that sense of distance is just right.
