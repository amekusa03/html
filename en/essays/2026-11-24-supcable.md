# The Car Audio Rabbit Hole, Part 8: Are Power Cables Really That Important?

2026-11-24

## The "Power Supply" Hurdle I Should Have Addressed Before Talking About Subwoofers

To be honest, I probably should have written about this power cable before my previous post on the subwoofer.
That’s because when you try to set up a subwoofer or an external amplifier, the first major problem everyone faces is **“where to get the power from”**.

When you search online for DIY car audio information, you’ll find the same advice everywhere: “If you’re installing an amplifier, you absolutely must power it directly from the battery!”
The reason is clear: the thin, factory-installed constant-power wire running to the back of the navigation system or audio unit (behind the head unit) simply lacks the power—specifically, the current-supply capacity—needed to drive an amplifier that demands a high current for even a split second.

I’ve even seen people warning, “If you splice off branches from the original thin wire, it’ll overload, generate heat, and in the worst case, the cable could catch fire!”
Theoretically, that’s certainly true, but in reality, in most cases the vehicle’s fuse will blow with a “pop” before the wires catch fire, and you’ll rarely see a case where the car burns to the ground. That said, considering the excessive strain on the factory wiring and the risk of audio distortion or unstable operation due to voltage drops, there’s no doubt that tapping into the wiring behind the navigation system should be avoided.

## The Ideal of “Direct Battery Replacement” and the High Hurdles Facing DIY Enthusiasts

Of course, the "direct battery connection"—which involves running a dedicated, thick cable from the battery's positive terminal into the car—is the most ideal and reliable standard method, both in terms of power supply and sound quality.

……But for DIY enthusiasts who like to do the work themselves, installing a battery booster is an incredibly daunting task.
The thick steel plate (bulkhead) separating the engine compartment from the passenger compartment has rubber grommets (gaskets) designed to route the factory wiring harness, but forcing a thick power cable through them is a real brain-teaser and the ultimate test of brute strength.
Scraped hands are a daily occurrence, and if you accidentally tear the rubber and create a gap, there’s even the nightmare risk of rainwater seeping into the cabin on a rainy day and flooding the floor.

## The Fuse Box: A Practical Workaround

So, what should you do if you’re intimidated by the difficulty of a direct battery connection?
The practical solution I’d like to recommend is to **“draw power from the fuse box inside the car.”**

Take a look inside the fuse box—you’ll find the cover under the driver’s seat or in a similar location.
You might find that slots for features like “seat heaters” or “sunroofs”—which shouldn’t be in your car—are, for some reason, connected to wiring or a backup power source.
This is because manufacturers dislike the cost of creating separate wiring harnesses for different trim levels or cold-weather specifications, so they use a common wiring system instead.

Since these are originally intended to power heating elements and motors, they are often equipped with high-amp fuses rated at 20A to 30A. For a typical slim-profile powered subwoofer (with a current draw of about 10A to 15A), simply tapping into this power source is more than enough to meet its needs.

To be honest, the subwoofer in my beloved car also draws power from a slot in the fuse box labeled “SUNROOF.” Even though it’s a mass-market car with absolutely no sign of ever having a sunroof, I’m still endlessly grateful for that thick main power line intended for the sunroof.

## The gauge of an electrical wire is determined by "current," not "voltage."

Now that you’ve decided where to plug in the power, the next question is, “What kind of cable should you use?”
A common misconception here is thinking, “I have a spare household extension cord lying around, so I’ll just use that.”

"Since household power cords can run home appliances ranging from 800W to 1,500W at 100V, a car subwoofer that uses only about 100W should be no problem at all"—I completely understand why you might think that. But you absolutely must not do this.

It is **current (amps)**—not voltage (volts)—that determines the wire gauge (the maximum current that can safely flow through the wire without overheating).
Think back to the formula you learned in middle school science: “Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A).”

- **When using an 800-watt appliance on a household (100V) circuit:**
  $$800\text{W} \div 100\text{V} = 8\text{A}$$
- **When powering a subwoofer that draws only 120W from a car (12V) battery:**
  $$120\text{W} \div 12\text{V} = 10\text{A}$$

That's right—in a car's extremely low-voltage 12V environment, even just putting out 120W of power causes a "high current" to flow—one that's greater than that of a 800W household heater. If you were to run a 200W amplifier at full volume, a high current of 16A or more would surge through the wiring.

In other words, you’ll need cables that are much thicker than standard household wiring.
When purchasing wiring, it’s best to check that the gauge (indicated by standards such as “square” or “AWG”) matches the maximum current it will carry (the amplifier’s rating or maximum current draw) and to choose OFC (oxygen-free copper) cable, which has low electrical resistance and is designed for audio use.

## The Joy of Paying Attention to the Invisible Foundation

Running a thick power cable under the carpet and neatly tucking it away so it’s out of sight is, to an outsider, a truly mundane and laborious task.
However, the sound produced by an audio system with a solid, thick “artery” (the power supply) is completely different—both in the punch of the deep bass and the clarity of the sound.

"Put your heart and soul into the parts you can't see"—if you start to find pleasure in this, you're already a full-fledged resident of the "swamp."
