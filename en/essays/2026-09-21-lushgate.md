# A Story About Placing a Microcomputer in a Mountain Valley Field to Wait for Rain

September 21, 2026 / ESP32 / IoT / Hardware

## background

When you step into the open-air fields and home gardens nestled in the mountains, you’ll find the “laws of nature”—unrelated to the conveniences of city life—unfolding before you.

Water is drawn from nearby springs and rainwater tanks; there is no power grid, and of course, household Wi-Fi signals don’t reach the area.

During the summer or when there are consecutive days of sunny weather, vegetables get parched in the blink of an eye. However, it’s difficult to visit the garden every single day without fail. You might think, “Why not just use a store-bought watering timer?” but inexpensive timers have a major drawback.

Even though we just had a sudden downpour yesterday, the sprinklers still mercilessly start splashing water all over the place at the scheduled time.

In off-grid environments with limited water resources, wasteful watering directly leads to the depletion of rainwater tanks. However, many commercially available “smart agriculture IoT” systems require constant cloud connectivity—such as via LTE or Wi-Fi routers—which results in high monthly communication costs.

If there's no cell service or power, you should set up an autonomous system that allows you to assess the situation on your own and quickly handle things on the spot using your smartphone.

With that in mind, I began designing **"LushGate"**, a fully off-grid automatic sprinkler system based on the ESP32-C3.

## "How to Tell If It's Raining" Without Melting the Electrodes

The key to automatic irrigation lies in how accurately it can determine whether it has rained recently.

However, if you leave a commercially available rain sensor module outdoors, the electrodes will undergo electrolysis (electrochemical corrosion) in just a few weeks, causing them to dissolve and crumble until they become unusable. This is because, in a DC circuit that is constantly energized, the ions in water droplets cause the metal to corrode rapidly.

Therefore, LushGate adopted **"symmetric, polarity-reversed pulse measurement"**.

- Two stainless steel (SUS) electrode plates are used.
- Measurements are taken once every few minutes, with the current flowing for only a few milliseconds.
- Alternate between HIGH and LOW (current direction) on the GPIO with each cycle.

By keeping the average DC voltage across the electrodes at nearly zero, electrolysis is prevented in principle. During standby, the microcontroller is put into Deep Sleep mode to maintain ultra-low power consumption, waking up briefly at set intervals (default: 3 minutes) to detect rain.

Instead of just checking whether it's "raining now," the system calculates "how many minutes it rained continuously over the past 24 hours" and, at the 7:00 a.m. decision time, intelligently skips watering, determining, "It rained enough yesterday, so there's no need to water today."

## Galvanic Isolation to Block Motor Noise

The irrigation system is powered by an affordable 3V kerosene pump available at home improvement stores, and the power is supplied by a 12V lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) battery charged by solar panels.

One problem that every electronics hobbyist has to grapple with at least once is **“the issue of microcontrollers malfunctioning or going haywire due to back-EMF noise from motors.”**

Intense surge noise is generated when the pump starts or stops. If you directly connect the control-side GND of the ESP32 to the drive-side GND of the motor, the noise will feed back into the system, causing the microcontroller to reset or, in the worst case, the chip to burn out.

Therefore, I inserted a **photocoupler isolation module** between the control circuit (3.3V ESP32) and the drive circuit (12V relay / 3V pump).

"Galvanic isolation" completely cuts off electrical connections and transmits signals solely through flashing lights. Precisely because this tool operates unattended for weeks on end in a field—a place where no one is watching—it has been designed to be exceptionally rugged.

## A Conversation in a Field Without a Router

The most troublesome aspect of IoT devices is “making configuration changes on-site.”
If you add an LCD display or numerous control buttons to the main unit, the difficulty of achieving waterproofing (such as IP65) for the case increases dramatically, which can lead to water damage.

"Let's limit the buttons to just the single BOOT button on the unit itself. We can just use the smartphones of the people on site as temporary displays."

Press and hold the BOOT button through the case for 2 seconds, and the device will boot up as a SoftAP (Wi-Fi access point). Simply connect your smartphone to the Wi-Fi network and open `http://lushgate.local` in your browser to access the modern web UI.

- **One-Tap RTC Time Synchronization**: Instantly calibrate your smartphone's internal clock from your browser.
- **Irrigation Parameter Settings**: Intuitively adjust irrigation times, rainfall thresholds, and pump ON/OFF duty cycles, then save them to NVS (non-volatile memory).
- **View Watering History & Download CSV**: Check the operation history for the last 60 sessions on the spot and take it with you.
- **Manual Testing**: A 30-second test run of the pump to verify wiring, and real-time verification of raw sensor data.

Once the setup is complete, either press “Go to Sleep” or leave it alone for five minutes, and the Wi-Fi will quietly shut down, returning once again to the silence of Deep Sleep. No router or internet connection is needed. It’s a direct dialogue between the fields and the people.

## Waiting for Parts: A Time of Abundance

Currently, LushGate is in the **"parts on order" stage of production**.

I'm currently waiting for the arrival of photocoupler modules, relays, step-down DC-DC converters, SUS plates, and other items I ordered from AliExpress and electronics parts stores.

The period before inserting components into the breadboard might be the most exciting time for an engineer. They go over the circuit diagram in their head again and again, write the C code for the firmware, and fine-tune the CSS gradients and microinteractions of the web UI running in their browser.

Once the actual parts arrive, I plan to first conduct a mock-up test on my desk, then assemble them into a waterproof box and send them out to a sun-drenched field nestled in the mountains.

It embraces the whims of natural rain, stores energy from sunlight, and quietly nourishes the earth only when needed.  
We eagerly await the day when the first drop from “LushGate” is poured onto the fields.
