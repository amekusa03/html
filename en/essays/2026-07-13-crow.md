# Regarding the relationship between mowing and crows flying

2026.07.13 / Engineering & Nature Notes

## observation

When you cut grass on your farm, there is a high possibility that you will be attacked by crows right after that. This seems to be a common phenomenon, and many similar reports can be found on farmers' blogs online. The most common reason for this is that when grass is cut, insects on the ground become visible, and birds come to hunt for them.

## discomfort

However, crows are not birds that like to eat small insects. Even though they are omnivorous and will eat anything, it is hard to imagine that their main target is small insects on the ground, as they go out of their way to gather just after mowing the grass.

## hypothesis

So I came up with a hypothesis. The idea is that what the crows are responding to is not the presence of insects themselves, but rather changes in the environment.

By mowing:

- The ground surface is exposed and visibility is improved (hidden frogs, rats, large insects, crop residue, etc. become easier to see)
- The very fact that something has changed is a signal for reconnaissance and exploration.

For crows, mowing may not function as ``food itself,'' but ``a sign that the feeding area may have been updated.''

## circumstantial evidence

There are some general findings that seem to support this.

- Crows have excellent eyesight and are said to be sensitive to movement, reflections of light, and changes in the ground surface.
- The fact that crows respond sensitively to environmental changes, such as thorough garbage management and a decrease in nesting sites, is cited as proof of the high intelligence of crows.
- Crows have a strong learning ability, remembering successful experiences in specific locations, and tend to repeatedly visit the same location and time.

All of these seem to be more consistent with the explanation that they detect changes and engage in exploratory behavior than with the explanation that they are searching for insects.

However, to date, no research has been found that quantitatively shows the correlation between mowing frequency and the number of crows visiting. It seems that there is still no data that can be expressed in terms of correlation coefficients such as saturation difference (VPD).

## Verification method (draft)

I wondered if it was possible to verify this hypothesis using data.

- Record the date and time of mowing
- Record the number of crows flying in and the length of time they stay after mowing the grass.
- Categorize the behavior of crows by whether they are ``pecking at the ground (looking for insects)'' or ``walking around and looking around (scouting).''

With this, you may be able to judge based on your own data whether the ``insect attraction theory'' or the ``state change detection theory'' is superior.

## summary

Although it is still at the hypothesis stage, I personally feel that the possibility that crows are reacting to ``change itself'' makes more sense, given the intelligence and sharpness of their vision, than the simple diagram that cutting grass = insects appear.
